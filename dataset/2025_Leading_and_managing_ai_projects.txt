Introduction 
Artificial intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing everything—from diagnosing diseases and 
saving lives to catching financial fraud and supercharging manufacturing practices. But 
there is a paradox: While technology races ahead, most AI projects still stall or fail. Why? 
It is not the technology. It is not the talent. It is the outdated project management models 
that cannot keep up with AI’s unique rhythm. 
AI projects succeed when they have clear structure, governance, and repeatable 
processes tailored to data-driven development. Traditional methods alone do not address 
AI’s need for iterative improvement, data readiness, ethical oversight, and continuous 
monitoring. 
The PMI Certified Professional in Managing AI (PMI-CPMAI)™ framework bridges this gap. 
It builds on proven project management development approaches like adaptive/agile and 
other data-centric methods while adding AI-specific practices that traditional frameworks 
lack. PMI-CPMAI™ keeps the iteration-focused methods and responsiveness of adaptive 
approaches and combines them with structured data management, governance, and 
business alignment throughout every project phase. 
 The PMI-CPMAI™ framework equips project professionals with practical tools to lead AI 
initiatives that deliver real business value and converts AI’s complexity into manageable 
phases that ensure clarity, accountability, and trustworthiness.

The High Failure Rate of AI Projects 
Despite major investments and rapid advances, most AI projects fail. Studies show that 
more than 80% of AI initiatives do not meet their goals, almost double the failure rate of 
typical IT projects. In 2025, S&P Global reported that 42% of companies scrapped most 
of their AI projects before completion, up from 17% the year before. NTT DATA found 
70%–85% of generative AI (GenAI) efforts fail to deliver expected ROI, while Gartner 
reports that only half of AI projects reach production, with many abandoned due to poor 
data, rising costs, or unclear value. 
These consistent findings highlight a clear problem: Technology alone is not enough. The 
biggest reasons for failure include weak business alignment, poor data readiness, lack of 
robust validation, and no plan for continuous improvement as data and needs evolve. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 
Where Traditional Project Management 
Frameworks Fall Short 
Traditional project management methods remain essential. Predictive, adaptive, and hybrid 
approaches provide strong foundations for managing scope, stakeholders, risks, and 
teams. Agile approaches may often be applied to help teams adapt and iterate as 
requirements change, which suits AI’s experimental nature. 
However, AI projects can bring unique challenges that general project management 
frameworks may not fully address. Unlike standard software, AI is driven by data that 
should be validated, prepared, governed, and continuously updated. Models should be 
tested and monitored long after deployment to ensure they remain accurate and ethical. 
Traditional project management approaches often assume stable requirements and clear 
deliverables. However, AI projects typically demand iteration and continuous monitoring— 
not just on features but on the underlying data and models. Older, data-focused methods 
like CRISP-DM do not cover modern AI’s complexity, including governance, ethics, and 
continuous oversight. 9, 10, 11

Project professionals require a proven approach that builds on traditional project 
management methods while adding AI-specific structure. PMI-CPMAI™ does exactly that, 
combining established project management strengths with clear, repeatable practices for 
data readiness, model validation, ethical AI, and continuous monitoring. 
The result is an end-to-end framework that helps AI projects succeed.

PMI-CPMAI™ in Context 
AI’s Rapid Rise in Projects 
Artificial intelligence has moved from niche technology teams to mainstream business 
operations, fundamentally changing how projects are delivered across industries. Global AI 
adoption has soared from 20% in 2017 to 77% in 2024, with GenAI usage more than 
doubling in 1 year alone. McKinsey reports that 72% of organizations use AI in at least one 
business function, and most senior leaders expect AI to significantly reshape project 
management in the next 5 years. 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 
AI Is Reshaping Project Work 
AI is now part of core project functions: automating documentation, improving risk analysis, 
optimizing resources, and streamlining stakeholder communication. IT operations, 
marketing, sales, and service teams are the main adopters, but sectors like manufacturing, 
healthcare, finance, and even retail and construction are accelerating AI use. However, 
there is a critical skills gap: Less than one-quarter of project managers have strong AI 
experience while nearly half have little to none. As AI automates routine tasks, project 
leaders must shift toward higher value work like coaching teams and managing 
stakeholders. 
AI as a Competitive Edge 
AI is now a strategic advantage, not just an efficiency tool. Organizations using AI should 
see significantly higher returns on investment (ROI) and faster scaling of products and 
services. By 2030, AI will increasingly drive project selection, progress tracking, and 
performance reporting. 
The GenAI Impact 
Generative AI’s user-friendly tools make advanced AI accessible to nonexperts, 
transforming project work through quick content generation, faster analysis, and smarter 
decision-making. PMI research shows GenAI can automate routine tasks, assist with 
moderately complex work, and augment high-level activities. 
Despite its promise for improving project work, managing GenAI projects brings new 
challenges, including data quality, bias, hallucination risks, intellectual property (IP) 
concerns, and the need for constant refinement. These challenges require updated 
approaches to scope, governance, and quality assurance.

Beyond GenAI 
The power of AI goes far beyond text and image generation. Computer vision, predictive 
analytics, natural language processing (NLP), autonomous systems, and optimization tools 
are transforming quality control, forecasting, contract management, and more. These 
applications often deliver more measurable business value than GenAI alone. 
Until recently, these areas demanded deep technical skills. Today, no-code and low-code 
platforms, automated machine learning (AutoML), and pretrained models are closing this 
gap, making more AI applications accessible to project teams. 
Scaling AI Success With the PMI-CPMAI™ Framework 
Quick wins with GenAI are only part of the opportunity. Long-term advantage comes from 
applying AI systematically across multiple functions. PMI-CPMAI™ provides a repeatable 
framework to do just that. 
Whether it is GenAI, computer vision, or predictive analytics, The six-phase approach of 
PMI-CPMAI™ helps ensure teams stay aligned with business goals, prepare the right data, 
manage iteration, and deliver AI that works in the real world. 
Organizations that master this structured approach will be ready to capture AI’s full value 
by turning scattered experiments into sustainable, competitive advantage. 
The Seven Patterns of AI 
AI covers a wide range of applications, from chatbots to autonomous vehicles. Each use 
case has different data, risk, and requirements that standard IT project management does 
not address. Many projects blend multiple AI patterns, so understanding these patterns 
helps project managers plan realistically and manage risks. 
 The Seven Patterns of AI organize common AI use cases and clarify project demands. 
While the umbrella term “artificial intelligence” encompasses a vast range of applications, 
each pattern requires different considerations from data dependencies and complexities to 
risk considerations, creating fundamentally different project management challenges. 
Despite different systems all using AI, the specific differences in their applications have 
profound implications for project management approaches, with each pattern bringing 
distinct risks, ROI factors, complexities, tool requirements, stakeholder needs, team 
requirements, time-to-delivery expectations, data dependencies, third-party integration 
capabilities, and regulatory considerations. 
AI project management is challenging because traditional application development and IT 
project methods don’t fully apply to data-driven projects. Understanding this difference is 
crucial, as any AI initiative may involve one or more patterns—and most advanced 
applications combine multiple patterns to achieve the desired result. 
The Seven Patterns of AI provide a strategic framework for organizing AI approaches and 
accelerating project planning by highlighting similarities among projects within the same 
pattern. Each pattern groups projects with similar objectives, technology foundations, data 
requirements, and implementation needs, helping teams anticipate key steps and fill in the 
gaps for successful execution.

Automation Versus AI: Understanding the Fundamental 
Difference 
Automation and AI, despite their ability to work together, often solve problems differently. 
Automation performs repeatable tasks with fixed rules (like RPA or batch jobs) in stable 
environments. AI handles variability and complexity through perception (understanding 
data), prediction (forecasting outcomes), and planning (adapting actions). If a system 
perceives, predicts, or plans, it requires AI-specific management. 
AI-enhanced automation combines both: It can adapt to changing conditions, handle 
exceptions, and learn over time. For example, a simple call router follows static rules, 
whereas an AI-powered bot can understand natural language, gauge sentiment, and 
dynamically adjust responses. 
AI agents take this further. They perceive, decide, and act autonomously, often combining 
different AI capabilities. Multiple agents can collaborate in workflows—like a virtual 
assistant using NLP, knowledge retrieval, and recommendations to resolve customer 
issues. This orchestration solves complex tasks that no single AI model can handle alone. 
Agentic AI goes a step beyond this: Agents can optimize and refine workflows on their own, 
collaborating, adjusting to new conditions, and learning from results—transforming AI from 
a simple tool to an intelligent partner. 
Automation is not considered one of the Seven Patterns of AI because the goal of 
automation is repetition: performing the same task consistently and efficiently with limited 
human intervention. Traditional automation can be implemented through systems that 
don’t require AI, such as rule-based engines, workflow orchestration, RPA, scripted 
processes, and mechanical systems. 
These conventional automation approaches excel in stable environments with predictable 
inputs and well-defined processes. Examples include manufacturing assembly lines, 
scheduled batch jobs, or basic email autoresponders, which can represent automation in 
its purest form: reliable, repeatable, and efficient execution of predetermined steps.

AI agents strengthen the Seven Patterns by adding coordination and self-improvement: 
• Recognition agents can team up with conversational agents to explain what is detected. 
• Predictive and anomaly detection agents can spot when forecasts drift. 
• Hyper-personalization can use multiple agents for behavior tracking, content delivery,

• Autonomous and goal-driven systems can deploy agents to handle perception,

The three Ps of intelligence differentiate intelligent systems from traditional automation or 
deterministic, programmatic systems in the following ways: 
• Perception. Perception is the ability to sense, interpret, and understand information 
from the environment, including unstructured data like images, natural language, audio, 
or complex patterns. While automation systems typically work with structured, 
predefined inputs, intelligent systems can process and make sense of variable, 
ambiguous, or previously unseen information. 
• Prediction. Prediction is the capability to forecast future outcomes, anticipate patterns, 
or make inferences based on incomplete information. Traditional automation follows 
predetermined paths, but intelligent systems can project likely scenarios and adjust 
behavior based on probabilistic reasoning and learned patterns. 
• Planning. Planning is the capacity to devise strategies, sequence actions dynamically, 
and optimize approaches to achieve objectives in changing conditions. Unlike 
automation’s fixed workflows, intelligent systems can adapt their approach, consider 
multiple variables, and modify their execution strategy based on circumstances. 
If humans are the ones performing the perception, prediction, and planning—and 
machines are simply executing those tasks—then it is the human providing the intelligence 
and the machine simply performing the tasks. If machines are responsible for one or more 
of these functions, they exhibit characteristics of intelligence. These probabilistic, learning-
based capabilities are what add value beyond traditional automation. 
AI-Enhanced Automation: Addressing Variability and Complexity 
Traditional automation systems struggle in environments of continual variability, particularly 
when required to interact with variable inputs. This is where AI-enhanced automation 
becomes transformative. By incorporating intelligent capabilities, automation systems can 
handle exceptions, adapt to changing conditions, respond to unstructured inputs, optimize 
performance in real time, and learn from experience to improve future execution. 
As an example, a traditional, automated customer service system might route calls based 
on preset rules, but an AI-enhanced version can understand natural language, interpret 
customer sentiment, predict the most effective resolution path, and dynamically adjust its 
approach based on individual customer history and current context. 
AI Agents: Coordinated Intelligence in Action 
AI agents represent a significant evolution beyond the more simplistic prompt-response 
pattern of individual AI models. An AI agent is an intelligent system that can perceive its 
environment, make decisions, and take action to achieve specific objectives with some 
degree of autonomy. Unlike stand-alone AI models that perform single functions, AI agents 
can combine multiple AI capabilities and interact with systems, data sources, and other 
agents to accomplish complex tasks. 
AI agents can be chained together into workflows to perform tasks that require coordination 
across multiple specialties. For instance, a comprehensive customer-inquiry-resolution 
system might involve an NLP agent to understand the inquiry, a knowledge retrieval agent to 
find relevant information, a recommendation agent to suggest solutions, and a 
communication agent to formulate and deliver the response.

The Trustworthy AI Framework: A Structured 
Approach 
 To help, PMI-CPMAI™ includes a practical Trustworthy AI Framework, structured to build 
trust into AI systems by design, not by chance. The framework integrates five layers: Ethical 
AI, Responsible AI, Transparent AI, Governed AI, and Explainable AI, each reinforcing the 
others to make trust part of the entire AI life cycle. 
Five Layers of Trustworthy AI 
Ethical AI: Align AI with human values like fairness and inclusion. Define what fairness 
means, monitor bias in data and models, set clear rules to prevent discrimination, and 
embed ethical checks early. Engage diverse stakeholders up front. 
Responsible AI: Ensure accountability for AI outcomes. Set clear roles, build in human 
oversight, define escalation paths for failures, and maintain audit trails for traceability. 
Transparent AI: Make AI’s logic understandable. Document data sources and implement 
model reasoning to provide clear explanations of how models make decisions. This effort 
helps ensure stakeholders can see what AI is doing. 
Governed AI: Build robust controls with policies, audits, and risk management. Ensure 
standards, oversight, and change management are embedded into project planning. 
Explainable AI: Ensure stakeholders can interpret AI decisions. Design systems that provide 
understandable explanations from the start. Good explainability can’t just be added on later. 
Embedding Trustworthy AI in PMI-CPMAI™ 
Trust should be built in at every PMI-CPMAI™ phase, not treated as a compliance checklist. 
In Business Understanding (Phase I), practitioners should define ethics, accountability, 
transparency, governance, and explainability needs. In Data Understanding (Phase II), 
practitioners should check for bias, ensure transparency in data handling, and plan 
governance controls. These principles guide decisions, testing, deployment, and 
monitoring throughout all phases, turning trust into a continuous practice, not a one-time 
activity. 
A Living Framework for Continuous Improvement 
The Trustworthy AI Framework also serves as a recordkeeping and communication tool. 
The framework helps teams document decisions, track improvements, share trade-offs 
with stakeholders, and build lasting knowledge for future AI work. 
For project managers, this structure helps turn complex ethics and governance into actionable 
practices, ensuring that trustworthy AI becomes the standard—not just a statement on paper. 
Key takeaway: Trust is earned by design and repeated good practice. By integrating 
trustworthy AI into PMI-CPMAI™, project managers can deliver AI systems that work as 
intended, build stakeholder confidence, and align with the highest standards of ethical, 
responsible technology.

The Six Phases of PMI-CPMAI™ 
Because AI projects require specialized management and a defined context in which they 
operate, the core of PMI-CPMAI™ provides six iterative phases that create a practical, 
structured roadmap for delivering AI projects successfully. 
PMI-CPMAI™ is a framework, not a rigid checklist. The six phases—Business 
Understanding, Data Understanding, Data Preparation, Model Development, Model 
Evaluation, and Model Operationalization—follow a logical order based on real-world 
lessons from thousands of AI projects. The key is not simply completing each step in 
isolation, but understanding how they connect to prevent common pitfalls that often derail 
AI work. 
Key Principles of the PMI-CPMAI™ Methodology 
A Framework, Not Dogma 
It is important to see PMI-CPMAI™ as a flexible guide, not a fixed set of rules. While the 
framework offers structure and good practices, true success depends on how well it is 
adapted to the particular industry, technology, team skills, and business goals. 
The core principles do not change: Align business goals before technical work, confirm 
data readiness before building models, keep feedback loops active, and include 
trustworthy AI practices from start to finish. 
The phases follow a logical progression based on real-world success. Start with 
understanding the business, then understand the data. This understanding should be 
followed by model development, testing, and deployment. Skipping steps often leads to 
failure due to unclear goals, poor data, or models that do not perform in production. 
By applying the six phases of PMI-CPMAI™, practitioners can follow a tested method that 
turns AI’s complexity into clear, repeatable steps. This helps deliver AI that works, builds 
trust, and stays aligned with business needs. 
Iterative by Design 
PMI-CPMAI™ phases are built to loop back. For example, the Data Understanding phase 
might expose gaps that require refining the Business Understanding phase. The Model 
Evaluation phase might reveal issues that require more data preparation. This built-in 
iteration reflects the reality that AI development is full of uncertainty and discovery.

PMI-CPMAI™ Phase I: Business Understanding 
Determining Business Fit for AI 
Phase I helps ensure the problem is right for AI. Many projects fail due to forcing AI to fit in 
situations where traditional solutions work better. The project manager should confirm AI is

Pinpoint the True Need: Identifying Genuine Pain Points and 
Opportunities 
Go beyond the hype. Identify the core problem or opportunity by analyzing operations, 
bottlenecks, and customer pain points. Find measurable business challenges that AI can

Mapping Business Needs to AI Patterns: Translating 
Requirements Into Technical Strategy 
Once AI is justified, map the business problem to one or more of the Seven Patterns of AI. 
This mapping clarifies scope, technical needs, data requirements, risks, and likely tools. For 
example, personalization links to the Hyper-Personalization pattern and fraud detection 
links to the patterns and anomalies pattern. By connecting business requirements to the 
right pattern, practitioners can guide AI designs with clear technical strategies. 
Simplifying Project Planning Through Pattern Identification 
Patterns help structure planning by clarifying data needs, tools, risks, and industry 
practices. They inform make-or-buy decisions and realistic timelines. If multiple patterns 
apply, prioritization may provide higher value and lower risk. 
Managing Multipattern Projects: Prioritization and Scope Control 
Many projects combine patterns. To reduce risk, start with a single pattern and expand 
later. This approach manages complexity, speeds delivery, optimizes resources, and 
supports organizational learning. 
Defining ROI, Success Metrics, and Scope 
Set clear, measurable business goals such as cost savings, revenue growth, efficiency gains, 
or risk reduction. Define a minimum viable product (MVP) to deliver meaningful value fast and 
keep the scope realistic. Avoid vague goals like “use AI to improve service.” Be specific. 
ROI and Key Metrics: Establishing Clear Success Criteria 
The core of any AI project is clear, measurable success. Unlike traditional software, AI 
should prove value through established key performance indicators (KPIs). 
• Cost reduction metrics. Set specific savings goals (e.g., cut call center costs by 25% 
through lower handling times and headcount) and factor in implementation and upkeep 
costs. 
• Revenue growth objectives. Tie AI revenue gains with clear attribution (e.g., boosting 
average order value by 15% or cross-sell rates by 20%) and ensure they are trackable 
and realistic. 
• Time savings and efficiency gains. Quantify process speed increases or productivity 
boosts (e.g., cut invoice processing from 3 days to 3 hours or halve equipment 
downtime). 
• Risk minimization and compliance. Define risk and compliance targets (e.g., lowering 
false negatives by 40% in fraud detection or cutting regulatory breaches by 90%). 
All ROI goals should be SMART (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound). 
Vague goals are not enough.

from business alignment to technical feasibility. 
In AI, data is not just an input: It is the raw material that creates intelligence. The quality, 
quantity, accessibility, and relevance of the data set the limits for what AI can achieve. No 
algorithm or computing power can fix weak data foundations. 
This dependence brings unique management challenges, including the following: 
• Data availability. AI will not succeed if the needed data does not exist or cannot be 
accessed in time. 
• Quality risks. Poor data quality, which might be a minor concern in software, can make 
AI useless or risky. 
• Representativeness. If training data does not match real conditions, models will fail in 
production. 
• Timeliness. Data should reflect current realities. Outdated data causes models to break 
when deployed. 
Key Activities in PMI-CPMAI™ Phase II for Data Understanding 
PMI-CPMAI™ Phase II is the bridge between business goals and technical execution. Phase I 
defines what success looks like, whereas Phase II tests if success is possible with the 
available data. This testing should be confirmed before investing in data preparation or 
modeling. 
Phase II answers four critical questions: 
• Do we have the right data? The data should directly match the patterns the AI needs to 
learn. 
• Can we access it? Technical, legal, or organizational barriers may block needed data. 
• Is the quality high enough? AI is far less forgiving of poor data than traditional systems. 
• Do we know its limits and biases? AI amplifies any bias in training data, creating risks. 
These questions should be resolved up front. In AI, you cannot fix fundamental data 
problems midproject. Additionally, the AI pattern chosen in Phase I also shapes Phase II, 
since each pattern has different data needs, quality requirements, and risks. Knowing this 
helps teams target their data checks effectively.

The Compounding Impact of Preparation Quality

Enhancing and Augmenting Data for AI Learning 
In PMI-CPMAI™ Phase III, project practitioners should guide the transformation of raw data 
attributes into the representations that AI algorithms can effectively use for learning and 
prediction. The following aspects should be considered: 
• Feature creation. Raw data rarely includes the exact variables an AI system needs. New 
features like transaction velocity or spending shifts should be derived for tasks like fraud 
detection. 
• Encoding and normalization. Convert categorical data into numbers and normalize 
continuous variables to help ensure no single feature skews the learning process. 
• Dimensionality optimization. Use feature selection or reduction to keep only the most 
useful data, lowering complexity and avoiding overfitting. 
• Temporal feature engineering. For time-series data, create features like lags, rolling 
averages, or trend indicators to capture patterns over time. 
• Data labeling and annotation. Many AI models require labeled data. Decide how to 
label (i.e., internally, through vendors, crowdsourcing, or mixed methods) the data by 
balancing quality, cost, and security. 
• Data quality control frameworks. Implement quality checks, expert reviews, and 
iterative feedback loops to improve data preparation and labeling consistency over time. 
Synthetic Data Augmentation 
When data is limited or sensitive, synthetic data can safely expand training sets while 
preserving privacy and reducing bias. By generating artificial examples that reflect real 
data’s patterns—without using actual sensitive records—teams can boost data sets for AI 
patterns like computer vision or conversational AI, which need large, varied training data. 
Synthetic data should be validated to match real-world patterns and avoid artifacts that 
could mislead models. Quality controls should confirm the data mirrors original 
distributions and business logic. Balance is critical—too much synthetic data can cause 
models to perform well on artificial data but poorly in real-world use. 
Synthetic generation should be part of the automated preparation pipeline, with clear 
generation rules, validation steps, and quality checks. Labeling should verify synthetic 
examples are correct and consistent. Validation and documentation can ensure that real 
and synthetic data together build strong, representative training sets that models can trust 
long term. 
Pipeline Development and Automation 
Teams build extract, transform, load (ETL) or extract, load, transform (ELT) pipelines to 
automate all preparation steps, helping to ensure consistent, repeatable processing that 
supports iterative development. The following aspects should be considered: 
• Data versioning and management. Versioning systems track data set changes, 
support experiment reproducibility, and allow rollbacks when needed. 
• Quality monitoring implementation. Automated monitors detect data drift, anomalies, 
and pipeline issues that could affect model performance. 
• Scalability and performance optimization. Pipelines are optimized for current 
workloads and designed to scale with growing data volumes.

Establishing Data Governance and Compliance 
Phase III of PMI-CPMAI™ integrates data governance and compliance considerations 
directly into preparation processes, which helps ensure that ethical and legal requirements 
are built into data handling rather than addressed as afterthoughts. 
Privacy Protection and Anonymization 
Ensuring the privacy and confidentiality of private data is critical to not only establishing 
trust with the AI systems, but also maintaining required levels of compliance and legal 
regulatory obligations. The following steps should be taken as part of the Data Preparation 
phase of an AI project, prior to the use of any data in an AI system: 
• De-identification processes. Teams should implement systematic de-identification 
processes that protect individual privacy while preserving the statistical properties 
needed for AI training. This effort might include removing direct identifiers, generalizing 
specific values, or applying differential privacy techniques. 
• Consent and usage compliance. Data preparation should respect the consent and 
usage terms under which data was originally collected. Teams should establish 
processes to ensure that data usage aligns with privacy policies and regulatory 
requirements. 
• Cross-border data considerations. For organizations operating internationally, data 
preparation should consider cross-border data transfer restrictions and local privacy 
regulations that might limit how data can be processed or stored. 
Bias Detection and Mitigation 
A key element to maintaining trust in AI systems is ensuring that AI outputs are fair. Biased 
outputs that over-, under-, or misrepresent certain aspects of the data may not only result 
in AI outputs that don’t match the real-world usage, but also further skew delivery of 
products, services, and value in potentially unfair ways. To address these issues, the 
following steps should be taken to identify and mitigate bias in the data during the data 
preparation phase of an AI project: 
• Systematic bias assessment. Teams should conduct systematic analysis to identify 
potential biases in prepared data sets, including demographic representation, temporal 
coverage, and systematic gaps that might disadvantage particular groups. 
• Mitigation strategy implementation. When biases are identified, teams should 
implement mitigation strategies, such as data augmentation, resampling, or synthetic 
data generation, to create more representative training sets. 
• Fairness metrics integration. Preparation processes help integrate fairness metrics 
that can be used to evaluate model performance across different demographic groups 
and use cases. 
• Statistical profile verification. Teams should generate comprehensive statistical 
profiles of prepared data sets and compare them against expected characteristics and 
business logic to identify potential issues.

The Build-Versus-Buy-Versus-Integrate Decision 
A key decision in Phase IV is whether to build, buy, or integrate AI capabilities. This choice 
shapes development time, resource needs, maintenance demands, and long-term 
flexibility. PMI-CPMAI™ provides a structured way to decide based on the business context, 
technical needs, and capabilities confirmed in earlier phases. 
Custom Model Development 
Building models internally offers fuller control over behavior, performance, and intellectual 
property (IP) but demands strong expertise, significant computing resources, and longer 
timelines. This option fits best when unique data, specialized requirements, or a need for 
proprietary AI gives a competitive edge. 
Custom development is optimal when: 
• Data patterns are unique to the industry or operation. 
• Proprietary AI offers strategic advantages. 
• Compliance requires full control over the model. 
• Off-the-shelf solutions can’t meet performance needs. 
When building custom models, the following details should be considered: 
• Technical requirements assessment. Teams should have ML specialists, compute 
capacity, strong data infrastructure, and commitment to maintain and improve models. 
Feasibility checks in Phase I should have confirmed these are in place. 
• Resource and timeline considerations. Custom builds take more time and up-front 
cost but provide the highest flexibility for tuning, which can yield lasting competitive 
benefits when aligned with clear business strategies. 
Use of Off-the-Shelf and Pretrained Models 
Using pretrained models or off-the-shelf AI can greatly speed up development and cut 
resource needs while delivering advanced capabilities from leading providers. This 
approach works best when the data and use case align with well-established AI patterns. 
When adopting pretrained models, the following aspects should be considered: 
• Optimal integration scenarios. Pretrained models are often ideal when the AI pattern 
identified in Phase I aligns with widely available solutions and development speed is 
critical for competitive or operational reasons. They are also a good choice when 
internal technical expertise is limited or the use case does not require specialized, 
domain-specific capabilities that differentiate the organization from competitors. 
• Technology ecosystem evaluation. The availability and quality of pretrained models 
vary significantly across AI patterns in the following ways: 
• 
Recognition patterns often have extensive ecosystems of pretrained computer vision 
models available through cloud providers and open-source libraries. 
• 
Conversational patterns have abundant application programming interface 
(API)-based solutions, though domain customization may be required. 
• 
Autonomous systems have very limited pretrained options due to safety and liability 
concerns, typically requiring custom development approaches.

Deployment Models and Strategies 
One of the most critical Phase VI tasks is selecting the best deployment environment and 
infrastructure for AI operations. This decision impacts performance, cost, security, 
compliance, and ultimately the business value defined in Phase I. The deployment strategy 
should align with the AI pattern, operational needs confirmed in Phase V, and organizational 
constraints set throughout PMI-CPMAI™. 
Different AI patterns have different needs: Recognition models may need GPU-accelerated 
cloud setups; conversational models may need low-latency configurations; predictive 
models may run best on batch infrastructure; and hyper-personalization may demand 
real-time pipelines. 
On-Premises Deployment: Maximum Control and Compliance 
On-premises deployment gives maximum control over security and compliance, keeping 
sensitive data fully within organizational boundaries. This deployment option suits highly 
regulated industries like finance, healthcare, or government where data residency or 
confidentiality is critical. Costs become more predictable with capital investment, which 
can be cheaper in the long term for high-volume use cases. However, this approach 
demands strong internal technical expertise, robust infrastructure, and dedicated 
resources for ongoing support, security, and recovery. 
Public and Private Cloud Deployment: Flexibility and Scalability 
Public clouds like Amazon Web Services (AWS), Azure, and Google Cloud offer flexibility, 
scalability, and access to advanced AI services. Elastic scaling handles variable demand, 
pay-as-you-go models align costs with value, and providers manage maintenance. Private 
cloud options add extra security for industries where shared infrastructure is a concern. 
Many organizations combine both with hybrid strategies, keeping sensitive workloads on 
premises while using cloud resources for training or peak demand. 
Private Cloud and Hybrid Strategies 
Private cloud deployment combines cloud flexibility with enhanced security through 
dedicated infrastructure that is not shared with other organizations. This approach enables 
regulatory compliance that prohibits shared infrastructure while maintaining cloud 
operational benefits. 
Hybrid deployment strategies optimize across different aspects by combining on premises 
and cloud infrastructure. Organizations might process sensitive data on premises while 
utilizing cloud resources for training or peak capacity management. 
Edge Device Deployment: Real-Time Processing and Distributed Intelligence 
Edge deployment pushes AI models onto devices or distributed nodes, enabling real-time 
responses with minimal latency. This approach is crucial for autonomous vehicles, industrial 
robots, or remote Internet of Things (IoT) systems. Local processing boosts privacy and 
cuts bandwidth use, allowing operation in low-connectivity areas. However, edge 
deployment requires model optimization for limited computing power, robust tamper 
proofing, and efficient ways to monitor distributed devices without draining resources.

Operationalizing GenAI Solutions 
GenAI solutions bring unique challenges because they create variable, human-like content 
rather than static outputs. This content demands specialized governance, oversight, and 
quality control. 
Unique Operational Requirements for GenAI 
Generative systems require real-time quality checks to ensure outputs are appropriate, 
accurate, and on brand. Automated validation should verify facts, brand voice, compliance, 
and standards while maintaining speed and usability. Because outputs can vary widely, 
organizations need strong prompt engineering, temperature controls, and filtering to keep 
content creative yet predictable. Clear guidelines, style guides, and automated checks help 
maintain consistent voice and tone across all uses. 
Hallucination Detection and Mitigation 
Preventing hallucinations—plausible but false content—requires real-time fact checking, 
source attribution, and transparency. Citation systems and confidence indicators can help 
users judge reliability. Systems should gracefully handle uncertainty by flagging 
questionable content, adding disclaimers, or redirecting to verified sources. 
Infrastructure and Performance Considerations 
API-based GenAI should control token usage to manage costs. Monitoring token spend, 
optimizing prompts, and caching common responses can keep operations efficient and 
affordable. Good caching helps ensure reused content stays relevant and contextually 
accurate. 
Latency and Real-Time Performance 
Fast, consistent responses are critical for user experience. Techniques like progressive 
generation and streaming let systems deliver partial content quickly while finishing the 
response. Load balancing and elastic scaling handle fluctuating demands. 
Regulatory Compliance and Content Standards 
Generated content should meet all applicable rules—from truth-in-advertising to data 
privacy. Automated checks should validate compliance while maintaining speed. Industries 
like healthcare, finance, and law require specialized validation and clear audit trails for 
oversight.

 Applying PMI-CPMAI™ to agentic AI projects demands careful focus on decision boundaries, 
coordination protocols, safety measures, and governance to keep autonomous systems 
aligned with business goals. Success factors include setting clear decision authority 
and escalation paths from the start, building strong safety and intervention controls, 
ensuring continuous monitoring and governance, and supporting teams as they adapt to 
human–agent collaboration. 
Organizations should prioritize thorough testing and validation of agent behaviors, 
coordination, and safety systems. Managing agentic AI requires new oversight methods 
beyond traditional software. The structured, iterative approach of PMI-CPMAI™ is essential 
for guiding these projects, managing emergent behaviors, and ensuring alignment and 
trust as systems learn and adapt.
